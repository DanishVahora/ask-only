The premise

Every company promises to delete your data. None of them can prove they did.

That is not a complaint about corporate honesty — it is a theorem. Broadbent and Islam proved that certified deletion is impossible using classical information, because classical ciphertexts can be copied. Cooper et al. showed you cannot cleanly remove a fact from a model's parameters either. What industry calls proof of deletion is a NIST form with somebody's phone number on it.

So deletion is the wrong thing to ask for. You can never prove you deleted it. You can prove you never held it.

Receipts, ranked

  • "We deleted it." A claim by the one party it would incriminate.
  • A signed deletion receipt. Proves a key holder signed a claim. Not that the data is gone.
  • Structural absence. The storage layer refuses the query, and the only route to the plaintext was a capability that no longer exists.

AskOnly issues no deletion receipts. It never had to — the verifier was never given anything to delete.

How it works

Three systems, each doing the one thing only it can do.

  1. ENSv2 — who may ask what

    The question-set a verifier may ask is a role on an ENS name. Not a row in our database, not a checkbox — an on-chain role bitmap. Revoking a permission is revoking a role.

    A refused question never reaches your document.

    hasRoles(labelId, 1<<40, landlord) → false

  2. 0G Private Computer — the only reader

    A model inside an Intel TDX enclave reads a minimal extract and returns one bit. India's real documents are unsigned, unstructured scans; there is no ZK circuit for a photograph of a payslip. The enclave is the only universal adapter from real paper to a purpose-limited answer.

    Your employer, PAN and account number never enter it.

    5 of 16 lines sent · 0 figures returned

  3. Arkiv — until when

    The permission is an entity whose expiry the network enforces. Past its death block, queries do not return it and every operation on it fails. And because Arkiv emits an event when a record is extended but nothing when one dies, keeping a permission alive is a public signed act while letting it die is silent.

    Retention becomes the thing you have to explain.

    block 283073 queryable → 283075 gone

What we don't claim

The boundary, before you find it.

A privacy project that overstates is worse than one that does less. Here is every place AskOnly stops.

  • noWe do not erase bytes from history. Arkiv's docs do not claim it, so neither do we. Expiry means queries stop returning it and operations fail.
  • noThe enclave does not enforce our policy. 0G is an inference router, not a place to deploy code. Enforcement lives at the client and the verifier, and we say so.
  • no0G does not sign the answer. The Router exposes no signature endpoint — we probed eight path shapes with a live request id, all 404. So a receipt is bound by provider address, never by a signature we did not get.
  • noExpiry kills the capability, not knowledge. A landlord told "yes" still knows "yes". We shrink what is disclosed to one bit and make every bit accountable. Nothing makes knowledge revocable.
  • noThe TEE boundary is real but not absolute. You are trusting Intel TDX, the dstack image and 0G's gateway. TEE.fail happened in October 2025.

What we do claim

The verifier never receives the document. The enclave receives only the lines bearing on the permitted question. The question that was asked and the answer given are both recorded, bound to the asker's own signature. And the permission expires whether anyone is watching or not.

India's DPDP Rules require a consent manager that cannot read what it brokers, and a record of every sharing event. They require no integrity guarantee on that record at all. That gap is the whole opportunity.
